If you have read any of my [brewing posts](/brewing/), you know I love a good number. Original gravity. Final gravity. ABV. I log them every time, and if you have browsed my [recipes](/brewing/), you have seen them printed right on the page.

For most of my time brewing, I believed those numbers were the whole game. If the hydrometer said what it was supposed to say, I figured I had brewed well. It took me a surprisingly long time — and more mediocre batches than I want to admit — to learn that this is not what the numbers are for.

## My Numbers Were Great. My Mead Was Not.

When I started brewing, I gave the readings far too much weight. If the original gravity landed where I expected and the fermentation finished dry where a calculator said it should, I considered the batch a success before I ever tasted it.

The problem was that a lot of that mead did not come out great. And because every measurement looked right, I never knew quite how to fix it. Was the yeast wrong? The nutrition? The honey? I would go looking for a flaw in the process, but the process appeared flawless on paper.

That was the trap. I kept treating good numbers as proof of a good brew, so every time the taste disagreed with the hydrometer, I assumed the problem must be hiding somewhere else — instead of trusting my own mouth.

It was never that I was unable to fix a batch. It was that I did not believe my palate had the authority to call a batch flawed when the numbers were telling me everything was fine.

## The Video That Hit Home

Brian and Derica of [City Steading Brews](https://www.youtube.com/@csfermenstation) recently put out a video that articulated exactly this, and it struck home with me. Their point, in one sentence: **the numbers are there for replication — they do not mean your brew is good.**

Gravity readings tell you the *story of the fermentation*: how much sugar went in, how much is left, when the yeast stopped. That story matters enormously — but only after you have a batch worth repeating. If the mead under the airlock tastes thin, harsh, or just boring, a textbook-perfect gravity curve does not rescue it.

## Numbers Are a Map, Not a Score

Here is the mental shift that finally stuck for me.

- **What the numbers are for:** making *this* batch again. OG and FG are the recipe's coordinates — log them, and you can rebuild the same mead next month or next year.
- **What the numbers are not:** a judgment of quality. A reading can be identical for a brilliant batch and a lousy one, because gravity has never tasted anything in its life.
- **Where quality actually lives:** in the glass. Balance, aroma, body, that little voice that says "one more glass" — none of that shows up on a hydrometer.

Once I reframed it that way, my logs started working *for* me. The number is not a grade; it is the address of a batch I either want to visit again or never return to. My log entries slowly gained a new field that mattered more than any gravity reading ever did: **would I make this again?**

## How I Log Now

Everything above does not mean you should stop taking readings — you absolutely should, because they are the only way replication works. But log them for the right reason. Here is what changed in my own process:

- **Take the reading, then taste the sample.** The hydrometer sample is a free preview of the batch. I started actually evaluating it instead of just reading the scale and dumping it back. Some brewers don't return samples at all for sanitation reasons — I'd rather lose an ounce than a data point.
- **Write flavor words next to the gravity.** "FG 0.998, harsh finish" is worth twenty times more to future-me than "FG 0.998" alone. The combination is what makes a log useful.
- **Record what the numbers can't see.** Clearing time, aroma at racking, whether the honey character survived — the things that turn a recipe into a *repeatable* one.
- **Let the palate overrule the paperwork.** If a batch tastes wrong and the numbers look right, the numbers are missing something. That is a puzzle to investigate, not a verdict to accept.

## Perfect Numbers, Good Batch

These days I think of the numbers and the tastes as two different jobs. The numbers record the brew so it can be rebuilt. My palate decides whether it is worth rebuilding.

If you are newer to this than I was: do not wait as long as I did to learn it. Take the readings, write them down — and then trust what your mouth tells you. Your log is for making the good batches again. Your taste buds get the final vote.
