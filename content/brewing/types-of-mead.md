Before you mix your first must, it helps to know what kind of mead you are aiming for. Mead is much broader than "honey wine." Change the honey, add fruit, introduce smoke or chilies, or ferment the same recipe dry instead of sweet, and you end up with a different style entirely. In this first part of the **Intro to Making Mead** series I will walk through the main categories, what defines each one, and where I think a beginner should start.

## Traditional Mead

This is the baseline: honey, water, and yeast, with no fruit, spices, or other flavoring ingredients added. What you taste is what the honey and yeast created together. A traditional mead can be dry, semi-sweet, or sweet, and it can be still or sparkling, but the core requirement is that honey character carries the show.

Traditional meads are the best place to learn how your yeast and honey choices shape the final glass. Without fruit or spice to hide behind, every decision matters.

## Melomel

A melomel is any mead made with fruit. Berries, stone fruit, tropical fruit, and even dried fruit all count. The fruit contributes sugar, acid, tannin, and color, which makes melomels more forgiving than traditional meads — the fruit can smooth out rough edges in a young batch.

Within melomel there are a few common sub-styles:

- **Cyser** — mead made with apple juice or cider instead of some or all of the water. It sits somewhere between mead and cider.
- **Pyment** — mead made with grape juice. It can taste like a honey-kissed white or red wine depending on the grapes.
- **Blackberry, cherry, strawberry, blueberry meads** — these are all melomels, even if people rarely use the umbrella term.

I make a lot of melomels because fruit gives the batch instant personality.

## Metheglin

Metheglin is mead made with spices, herbs, or both. Cinnamon, cloves, ginger, vanilla, cardamom, chamomile, and lavender are common choices. The name comes from an old Welsh word for spiced mead, and the style is a great way to experiment once you are comfortable with a basic ferment.

The trick with metheglin is restraint. A little spice goes a long way in mead, and it is much easier to add more later than to remove too much.

## Braggot

Braggot is a hybrid of mead and beer. It is made with both honey and malted grain, and it is usually hopped. The malt contributes body, color, and bready or caramel notes, while the honey keeps the finish lighter and adds floral or fruity character.

I have not brewed a braggot myself yet, but it is on my list. If you come from a beer-brewing background, this style is a natural bridge into mead.

## Bochet

Bochet is made by caramelizing the honey before fermentation. Heating honey darkens it, deepens the flavor, and introduces notes of toffee, marshmallow, dark fruit, and even a slight bitterness. A bochet can be a traditional mead, a melomel, or a metheglin — the caramelized honey is what defines it.

Bochet takes extra care because hot honey expands and can boil over violently. It is not a beginner process, but the results can be stunning.

## Capsicumel / Capsumel

Capsicumel, sometimes written **capsumel**, is mead flavored with peppers — usually chili peppers. The heat can range from a gentle warmth to genuinely spicy, and the pepper flavor is usually balanced with fruit, smoke, or honey sweetness.

I have not brewed one yet, but I am fascinated by them. Capsicumels are a great example of how flexible mead can be; the honey base supports flavors that would fight each other in other beverages.

## Hydromel / Session Mead

A hydromel is a lower-alcohol mead, often in the 3.5% to 7% ABV range. The name comes from the Greek root for water, and the style is intentionally light, crisp, and drinkable. Session meads are usually carbonated and served cold, more like a hard cider or sparkling wine than a heavy dessert mead.

Hydromels are a good choice if you want something refreshing and do not want to wait months for a high-ABV batch to mellow.

## Sack Mead / Dessert Mead

At the opposite end of the spectrum, sack mead is strong and sweet, typically 14% ABV or higher with plenty of residual honey left in the finished product. The higher alcohol and sugar make it rich, warming, and suited to small pours after dinner.

Sack meads take patience. They often need a year or more of aging before the alcohol heat mellows and the flavors integrate.

## Sparkling Mead

Any of the styles above can be sparkling. Carbonation adds mouthfeel, lifts aromatics, and can make a dry mead feel more complete. Sparkling meads are bottled with a small amount of priming sugar and allowed to carbonate naturally, or they are force-carbonated in a keg.

I usually bottle still meads, but I have been tempted to try force-carbonating a session hydromel.

## Smoked and Specialty Meads

Beyond the classic categories, mead makers add everything from coffee and chocolate to flowers, tea, and smoke. A **bochet** with smoked malt becomes something close to a braggot-bochet hybrid. A **metheglin** with lapsang souchong tea picks up a campfire quality. There is no firm style name for every combination, and that is part of the fun.

When you move into specialty territory, the best approach is to pick one dominant flavor idea and build the rest of the recipe around it.

## Where to Start

If this is your first batch, I recommend a **traditional mead** or a simple **melomel**. Both teach you how honey, yeast, water, and time interact, and they give you a baseline to compare against when you start experimenting. A traditional mead will show you exactly what your yeast and honey contributed. A melomel with berries or cherries will be more forgiving and usually ready to drink a little sooner.

Once you have a few batches under your belt, try a **metheglin**, experiment with a **bochet**, or split a batch in secondary to test two different flavor additions side by side.
