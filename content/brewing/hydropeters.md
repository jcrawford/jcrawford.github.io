If you brew, you need one. It is called a **hydrometer** — hydro for water, meter for measuring — and it is the tool that tells you how much sugar is in your must and what the yeast has done with it. Every gravity reading on every recipe on this site came out of this one slender glass tube.

Around here, though, I have started calling them **hydropeters** — hydro for water, peter after the little weighted beads they scatter across the floor when one finally goes. The header photo for this post is the reason. That is my most recent one, snapped clean at its narrow waist next to the black shot that used to hold it upright in the sample cylinder. It looks almost whole in that picture. It absolutely is not.

## Three Years, One Drop

By the numbers, I have been lucky: in three years of brewing I have dropped exactly one hydrometer. One. I handle that thing like it is already broken — into its case after every use, lowered into the sample cylinder between two fingers instead of dropped in, never loose in a drawer or a brew bag — and it still only took one small slip to end it. No clatter, no drop I could even point to afterward. Just a moment between safe and used, and then the thin glass at the waist decided it was done.

That is the part worth understanding about these tools: the fragility is not a defect that a better brand fixes. It is built in. A glass hydrometer is thin precisely so it floats to a readable depth, weighted with shot precisely so it stands upright, tapered because that shape is what the measurement needs. Every single feature that makes it a good instrument makes it terrible at surviving contact with the tile floor. And when it goes, there is no repair and no battery to swap — there is ordering a new one and spending two or three days mid-ferment with nothing watching the batch.

Which is why, somewhere between the first broken hydrometer and the second hydrometer-shaped box on my shelf, the joke name stuck. They are not sturdy enough to deserve the dignity of reliable spelling. They break, so the name breaks.

## The Whole Protection Plan

It fits in one sentence. The hydrometer lives in its plastic case, it only ever meets a proper sample cylinder, it goes in lowered gently — never dropped — and a spare sits on the shelf waiting for the day my luck runs out again. That is it. No gadget, no upgrade kit, no ruggedized version worth buying. Cheap, essential glass, one careless inch from being gravel.

If you want to see what those delicate little readings actually do for a batch, every recipe on my [brewing page](/brewing/) carries its gravity numbers straight off a hydrometer — [the cherry mead](/brewing/cherry-mead/) I just bottled ran from a 1.099 starting gravity down to a bone-dry 1.000 before it earned its juice. Buy two. Keep one cased. And enjoy the floor beads.
