## What Is Water Chemistry?

Water chemistry is the practice of understanding and adjusting the mineral content of your brewing water to achieve specific flavor outcomes. It's the difference between mead that tastes flat and inconsistent, and mead that sings with clarity, balance, and intention.

Honey is mostly sugar and water. Yeast does the fermentation work. So why does water matter? Because the minerals dissolved in your water directly affect:

- **Yeast health and fermentation performance** — certain ions are essential nutrients
- **pH of the must** — which affects flavor extraction, yeast activity, and stability
- **Perceived sweetness and dryness** — some ions enhance sweetness, others accentuate dryness
- **Flavor clarity and finish** — the right balance makes flavors pop; the wrong one muddies them

## Why I Started Caring About Water Chemistry

For my first batches, I used tap water straight from the tap. Some batches turned out great. Others tasted off — dull, slightly sour, or just "wrong." I couldn't figure out the pattern until I started testing my water and tracking mineral additions.

The breakthrough came when I realized: **my tap water changes seasonally**. Municipal sources shift between groundwater and surface water throughout the year. My process was consistent; my water wasn't.

Once I started building water profiles from a neutral base (distilled or RO water) and adding minerals intentionally, my mead became consistent. Every batch. Same yeast, same honey, same process — but now the water was a variable I controlled, not a variable that controlled me.

## The Key Ions and What They Do

Water chemistry for mead revolves around six primary ions. Each serves a specific purpose:

| Ion | Symbol | Primary Role | Typical Range |
|-----|--------|--------------|---------------|
| Calcium | Ca²⁺ | Yeast nutrient, clarity, structure | 50–100 ppm |
| Magnesium | Mg²⁺ | Yeast nutrient, enzyme support | 5–20 ppm |
| Sodium | Na⁺ | Enhances sweetness, mouthfeel | < 20 ppm |
| Chloride | Cl⁻ | Fullness, roundness, sweetness | 50–150 ppm |
| Sulfate | SO₄²⁻ | Dryness, crispness, accentuates fruit | 50–150 ppm |
| Bicarbonate | HCO₃⁻ | pH buffer, prevents crash | 30–80 ppm |

**ppm** = parts per million (milligrams per liter)

These aren't arbitrary numbers. They're ranges that work for most mead styles. A dry traditional mead benefits from higher sulfate and lower chloride. A sweet fruit mead benefits from higher chloride and lower sulfate. The ratio matters more than the absolute values.

## The Chloride-to-Sulfate Ratio

This is the single most important concept in water chemistry for mead. The ratio between chloride and sulfate determines whether your mead tastes **full and sweet** or **dry and crisp**:

| Ratio (Cl⁻:SO₄²⁻) | Character | Best For |
|-------------------|-----------|----------|
| 1:1 | Balanced | Traditional dry meads |
| 2:1 | Fuller, sweeter | Sweet meads, fruit meads |
| 1:2 | Drier, crisper | Dry traditional, show meads |
| 3:1+ | Very full | Dessert meads, sack meads |

If your mead tastes thin or overly dry, increase chloride or decrease sulfate. If it tastes cloying or heavy, increase sulfate or decrease chloride.

## pH and Why It Matters

Target pH for mead must: **3.2–4.2**. Ideal range: **3.4–3.8**.

Why?

- **Below 3.2**: Yeast struggles, fermentation stalls, flavors become sharp
- **Above 4.2**: Bacteria can thrive, flavors become dull, instability increases

Bicarbonate is your primary pH buffer. It prevents the pH from crashing too low during fermentation. But too much bicarbonate raises the starting pH and can make the mead taste chalky.

I test pH at three points: after mixing the must, 24 hours into fermentation, and at bottling. If the initial pH is above 4.0, I add a small amount of acid blend or increase sulfate. If it's below 3.2, I add bicarbonate or reduce acidic fruit additions.

## Building Your First Water Profile

Here's my recommended starting point for a balanced traditional mead:

| Ion | Target ppm |
|-----|------------|
| Calcium | 75 |
| Magnesium | 10 |
| Sodium | 10 |
| Chloride | 100 |
| Sulfate | 100 |
| Bicarbonate | 50 |

This gives you a 1:1 chloride-to-sulfate ratio, solid calcium for yeast health, and moderate bicarbonate for pH stability. Adjust from there based on the style you're making.

**Start with distilled or RO water.** Tap water is unpredictable. Well water is worse — often loaded with iron, sulfur, or excessive minerals that fight your adjustments. Distilled water is a blank canvas. You add exactly what you want, nothing you don't.

## My Setup

I keep small jars of each brewing salt on my mixing shelf, labeled with the ion and dosage rate. A cheap digital scale (0.01g resolution) lets me measure precisely. For a 1-gallon batch, I'm usually adding 0.4–2 grams of each salt depending on the target profile.

It sounds fussy. It's not. It takes 10 minutes to mix minerals into your strike water, and it's the difference between mead that's *good sometimes* and mead that's *great every time*.
